Overview

Core Idea

The female reproductive system shows regular cyclic changes that, teleologically, may be regarded as periodic preparations for fertilization and pregnancy. In humans and other primates this is the menstrual cycle, whose most conspicuous feature is periodic vaginal bleeding with shedding of the uterine mucosa (menstruation). The cycle has two linked parts, the ovarian cycle (follicular, ovulatory, luteal phases) and the uterine cycle (proliferative, secretory phases, then menstruation), controlled by the ovarian hormones estrogens and progesterone.

Learning Points

  • The menstrual cycle averages 28 days, counted from the first day of menstruation; its length is notoriously variable.
  • Ovarian cycle: follicular phase (dominant follicle forms, about day 6 onward) → ovulatory phase (about day 14) → luteal phase (corpus luteum secretes estrogen and progesterone).
  • Uterine cycle: proliferative phase (estrogen from the developing follicle) → secretory phase (estrogen + progesterone from the corpus luteum) → menstruation when the corpus luteum regresses.
  • The secretory phase is constant (about 14 days); variation in cycle length is mostly due to the proliferative phase.
  • If fertilization fails, the corpus luteum degenerates into a corpus albicans and the endometrium is shed; if pregnancy occurs, the corpus luteum persists and periods stop until after delivery.
  • Anovulatory cycles have no corpus luteum and no progesterone effect; unopposed estrogen thickens the endometrium until it sloughs.
  • Estrogens (17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol) are made by granulosa cells using androgens supplied by theca interna cells (via aromatase, CYP19).
  • Estrogens act on the genitalia, endocrine organs, breasts, and female secondary sex characteristics; progesterone acts mainly on the uterus, breasts, and brain.

Key Definitions

  • Menstrual cycle: the cyclic reproductive cycle of humans and other primates; its most conspicuous feature is periodic vaginal bleeding.
  • Menstruation: the periodic vaginal bleeding that occurs with the shedding of the uterine mucosa.
  • Primordial follicles: present under the ovarian capsule from birth; each contains an immature ovum.
  • Antrum: the cavity that forms around the ovum in an enlarging follicle (antrum formation); it is filled with follicular fluid.
  • Dominant follicle: the one follicle that grows rapidly (about the 6th day) while the others regress.
  • Atretic follicles: regressed follicles; the atretic process involves apoptosis. Atresia (involution) also removes primordial follicles before and after birth.
  • Ovulation: at about the 14th day the distended follicle ruptures and the ovum is extruded into the abdominal cavity.
  • Corpus hemorrhagicum: the follicle that ruptured at ovulation, promptly filled with blood.
  • Mittelschmerz: fleeting lower abdominal pain from peritoneal irritation by minor bleeding from the follicle into the abdominal cavity.
  • Corpus luteum: formed when granulosa and theca cells proliferate and the clotted blood is replaced by yellowish, lipid-rich luteal cells.
  • Luteal phase: the phase that begins with corpus luteum formation, during which the luteal cells secrete estrogen and progesterone.
  • Corpus albicans: the scar tissue that replaces a degenerated corpus luteum.
  • VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor): evidence indicates it is essential for development of the corpus luteum's blood supply.
  • Proliferative phase: the part of the cycle in which the endometrium regrows and thickens (proliferative changes); also called the preovulatory or follicular phase of the cycle.
  • Secretory phase: the part of the cycle in which the endometrium is highly vascularized, slightly edematous, and its glands secrete; also called the luteal phase.
  • Anovulatory cycle: a menstrual cycle in which ovulation fails to occur.
  • Estrogens: steroids; the naturally occurring ones are 17β-estradiol, estrone, and estriol.
  • Aromatase (CYP19): enzyme on which estrogen biosynthesis depends; converts testosterone to estradiol and androstenedione to estrone.
  • Feminizing hormones: the estrogens, responsible in part for female secondary sex characteristics.
  • Female escutcheon: the characteristic flat-topped pattern of female pubic hair.
  • "Estrogen-dominated" uterus: uterus under estrogen influence; more excitable and more sensitive to oxytocin.
  • Postcoital ("morning-after") contraception: large doses of estrogens for 4–6 days after coitus during the fertile period.
  • Progesterone: a steroid secreted by the corpus luteum, the placenta, and (in small amounts) the follicle.
  • Pregnanediol: the liver metabolite of progesterone, conjugated to glucuronic acid and excreted in urine.

Classification / Types

1. Components of the menstrual cycle (Figure 22–12)

  • Ovarian cycle: follicular phase → ovulatory phase → luteal phase.
  • Uterine cycle: menstrual (about days 1–5) → proliferative (to about day 14) → secretory (about days 15–28) → menstrual.
  • Naming equivalents: proliferative = preovulatory = follicular phase of the cycle; secretory = luteal phase.

2. Ovarian structures (Figure 22–11)

  • Follicle stages: primordial follicle → antral follicle → mature follicle (with antrum); atretic follicle (regressing).
  • Follicle wall layers: theca externa, theca interna, granulosa, and follicular fluid.
  • After ovulation: ovulated oocyte, corpus hemorrhagicum, young corpus luteum, mature corpus luteum, regressing corpus luteum, corpus albicans.
  • Other labelled structures: germinal epithelium, ovarian stroma, interstitial cell mass, blood vessels.

3. Natural estrogens

  • 17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol.

4. Sources of estrogen

  • Granulosa cells of the ovarian follicles (primary source of circulating estrogen), the corpus luteum, and the placenta.
  • Estrone formation from androstenedione also occurs in fat, liver, muscle, and brain.

5. Estrogen effects

  • On the female genitalia; on endocrine organs; on the breasts; on female secondary sex characteristics.

6. Sources of progesterone

  • Corpus luteum, placenta, and (small amounts) the follicle; small amounts also enter the circulation from the testes and adrenal cortex.

7. Principal target organs of progesterone

  • Uterus, breasts, and brain.

8. Plasma binding

  • Estradiol: free, albumin, gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG).
  • Progesterone: free, albumin, corticosteroid-binding globulin.

Important Concepts

The menstrual cycle

  • Cyclic changes of the female reproductive system are periodic preparations for fertilization and pregnancy.
  • Average length 28 days from the start of one period to the start of the next; days are numbered from the first day of menstruation.

Ovarian cycle: follicular phase

  • From birth there are many primordial follicles under the ovarian capsule, each with an immature ovum.
  • At the start of each cycle several follicles enlarge and form an antrum filled with follicular fluid.
  • About the 6th day one follicle grows rapidly and becomes the dominant follicle; the others regress as atretic follicles (apoptosis).
  • How the dominant follicle is selected is uncertain; it seems related to the follicle's ability to secrete the estrogen inside it that is needed for final maturation.
  • The granulosa cells are the primary source of circulating estrogen, but theca interna cells are necessary: they secrete androgens that granulosa cells aromatize to estrogen.

Ovarian cycle: ovulatory phase

  • At about the 14th day the distended follicle ruptures and the ovum is extruded into the abdominal cavity (ovulation).
  • The ovum is picked up by the fimbriated ends of the uterine tubes (oviducts), transported to the uterus and, unless fertilization occurs, out through the vagina.

Ovarian cycle: luteal phase

  • The ruptured follicle fills with blood (corpus hemorrhagicum); minor bleeding into the abdominal cavity may cause mittelschmerz.
  • Granulosa and theca cells proliferate; clotted blood is rapidly replaced by yellowish, lipid-rich luteal cells, forming the corpus luteum, which secretes estrogen and progesterone.
  • Corpus luteum growth depends on an adequate blood supply; VEGF is essential.
  • Pregnancy: the corpus luteum persists and usually there are no more periods until after delivery.
  • No pregnancy: it begins to degenerate about 4 days before the next menses (24th day) and is replaced by scar tissue, the corpus albicans.
  • In other mammals the ovarian cycle is similar, but in many species more than one follicle ovulates and multiple births are the rule. Corpora lutea form in some submammalian species but not in others.

Ova numbers

  • In humans no new ova are formed after birth.
  • Fetal ovaries contain over 7 million primordial follicles; many undergo atresia before birth and others after birth.
  • At birth: about 2 million ova. At puberty: fewer than 300,000 in both ovaries.
  • Only one of these ova per cycle (about 500 in a normal reproductive life) normally reaches maturity; the remainder degenerate.

Uterine cycle

  • At the end of menstruation all but the deep layers of the endometrium have sloughed. A new endometrium regrows under estrogens from the developing follicle, increasing rapidly in thickness from the 5th to the 14th day.
  • Uterine glands are drawn out and lengthen, but do not become convoluted or secrete to any degree. These changes are proliferative (proliferative / preovulatory / follicular phase).
  • After ovulation, under estrogen and progesterone from the corpus luteum, the endometrium becomes highly vascularized and slightly edematous; glands become coiled and tortuous and secrete a clear fluid (secretory or luteal phase).
  • Function: the proliferative phase is restoration of the epithelium after the preceding menstruation; the secretory phase is preparation of the uterus for implantation of the fertilized ovum.
  • The secretory phase is remarkably constant at about 14 days; variation in cycle length is mostly due to the proliferative phase.
  • Large quantities of prostaglandins are present in the secretory endometrium and in menstrual blood.
  • When the corpus luteum regresses, hormonal support is withdrawn: the endometrium becomes thinner, which adds to coiling of the spiral arteries; foci of necrosis appear and coalesce; spasm and degeneration of the spiral artery walls lead to spotty hemorrhages that become confluent and produce the menstrual flow. The vasospasm is probably produced by locally released prostaglandins.
  • When fertilization fails to occur in the secretory phase, the endometrium is shed and a new cycle starts.

Normal menstruation

  • Menstrual blood is predominantly arterial; only 25% is of venous origin.
  • It contains tissue debris, prostaglandins, and relatively large amounts of fibrinolysin from endometrial tissue. Fibrinolysin lyses clots, so menstrual blood does not normally clot unless the flow is excessive.
  • Usual duration 3–5 days (as short as 1 day, as long as 8 days in normal women).
  • Blood lost ranges from slight spotting to 80 mL; the average is 30 mL; loss of more than 80 mL is abnormal.
  • Flow is affected by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases that affect the clotting mechanism.

Anovulatory cycles

  • Ovulation fails to occur; such cycles are common for the first 12–18 months after menarche and again before the onset of menopause.
  • No corpus luteum is formed, so the effects of progesterone on the endometrium are absent.
  • Estrogens continue to cause growth; the proliferative endometrium becomes thick enough to break down and begins to slough.
  • Bleeding time is variable, usually less than 28 days from the last period; the flow ranges from scanty to relatively profuse.

Estrogens: synthesis, transport, fate

  • Steroids secreted primarily by the granulosa cells of ovarian follicles, the corpus luteum, and the placenta.
  • Aromatase (CYP19) converts testosterone → estradiol and androstenedione → estrone (the latter also in fat, liver, muscle, brain).
  • Theca interna cells have many LH receptors; LH acts via cAMP to increase conversion of cholesterol to androstenedione, which they supply to the granulosa cells.
  • Granulosa cells make estradiol when given androgens; in primates the estradiol is secreted into the follicular fluid.
  • Granulosa cells have many FSH receptors; FSH acts via cAMP to increase aromatase activity. Mature granulosa cells also acquire LH receptors, and LH also stimulates estradiol production.
  • Circulating estradiol: 2% free, 60% bound to albumin, 38% bound to GBG (the same globulin that binds testosterone).
  • In the liver, estradiol, estrone, and estriol are converted to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates and excreted in the urine. Appreciable amounts are secreted in the bile and reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation).

Estrogen secretion

  • Comes from the ovary, with two peaks: one just before ovulation and one during the midluteal phase.
  • After menopause, estrogen secretion declines to low levels.
  • Figure (hormone profile across the cycle): plots basal body temperature, inhibin A, inhibin B, progesterone, estradiol, LH, and FSH against days relative to the midcycle LH peak, separating follicular and luteal phases.

Effects of estrogens: female genitalia

  • Facilitate growth of the ovarian follicles; increase motility of the uterine tubes.
  • Increase uterine blood flow; the uterine smooth muscle becomes more active and excitable, with more frequent action potentials in individual fibers.
  • The "estrogen-dominated" uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin.

Effects of estrogens: endocrine organs

  • Estrogens decrease FSH secretion.
  • Under some circumstances they inhibit LH (negative feedback); under others they increase LH (positive feedback).
  • Postcoital estrogens: pregnancy is probably prevented by interference with implantation rather than changes in gonadotropin secretion.

Effects of estrogens: breasts

  • Produce duct growth and are largely responsible for breast enlargement at puberty; called the growth hormones of the breast.
  • Responsible for pigmentation of the areolas, although it usually becomes more intense in the first pregnancy than at puberty.

Effects of estrogens: female secondary sex characteristics

  • Body changes at puberty are due in part to estrogens (the "feminizing hormones") and in part simply to the absence of testicular androgens.
  • Enlargement of breasts, uterus, and vagina.
  • Narrow shoulders and broad hips, thighs that converge, arms that diverge (wide carrying angle).
  • The larynx retains prepubertal proportions, so the voice stays high-pitched.
  • Less body hair and more scalp hair; pubic hair has the flat-topped pattern (female escutcheon).
  • Growth of pubic and axillary hair in both sexes is due primarily to androgens, not estrogens.
  • This body configuration, plus the female fat distribution in breasts and buttocks, is also seen in castrate males.

Progesterone: source and fate

  • Secreted by the corpus luteum, the placenta, and (small amounts) the follicle.
  • An important intermediate in steroid biosynthesis in all tissues that secrete steroid hormones; small amounts enter the circulation from the testes and adrenal cortex.
  • Circulating progesterone: about 2% free, 80% bound to albumin, 18% bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin.
  • Short half-life; converted in the liver to pregnanediol, conjugated to glucuronic acid and excreted in the urine.

Progesterone: secretion

  • Plasma level in men about 0.3 ng/mL (1 nmol/L); in women about 0.9 ng/mL (3 nmol/L) in the follicular phase.
  • The difference is due to small amounts made by ovarian follicle cells: theca cells provide pregnenolone to granulosa cells, which convert it to progesterone.
  • Secretion begins to increase late in the follicular phase. In the luteal phase the corpus luteum produces large quantities and plasma progesterone peaks at about 18 ng/mL (60 nmol/L).
  • LH stimulates corpus luteum progesterone secretion by activating adenylyl cyclase, with a subsequent step dependent on protein synthesis.

Progesterone: actions

  • Target organs: uterus, breasts, brain.
  • Responsible for the progestational changes in the endometrium and the cyclical changes in the cervix and vagina.
  • Antiestrogenic effect on myometrial cells: decreases their excitability, sensitivity to oxytocin, and spontaneous electrical activity, while increasing their membrane potential.
  • Decreases the number of estrogen receptors in the endometrium and increases conversion of 17β-estradiol to less active estrogens.
  • Breast: stimulates development of lobules and alveoli; induces differentiation of estrogen-prepared ductal tissue and supports the secretory function of the breast during lactation.
  • Feedback: complex, exerted at both hypothalamic and pituitary levels. Large doses inhibit LH and potentiate the inhibitory effect of estrogens, preventing ovulation.
  • Thermogenic: probably responsible for the rise in basal body temperature at the time of ovulation.
  • Stimulates respiration (and alveolar PCO2).
  • Large doses produce natriuresis, probably by blocking the action of aldosterone on the kidney.
  • No significant anabolic effect.

Processes / Stages

Menstrual cycle in sequence (28-day cycle)

  1. Day 1: start of menstruation (shedding of the uterine mucosa).
  2. Follicular / proliferative phase: follicles enlarge; about day 6 one becomes dominant; estrogen rises and the endometrium regrows (rapid thickening day 5–14).
  3. About day 14: ovulation (follicle ruptures, ovum extruded).
  4. Luteal / secretory phase (about 14 days): corpus hemorrhagicum → corpus luteum → estrogen + progesterone → endometrium vascularized, edematous, glands coiled and secreting.
  5. About day 24 (no pregnancy): corpus luteum degenerates (about 4 days before menses) into a corpus albicans.
  6. Hormonal support withdrawn → menstruation; a new cycle starts.

Follicular phase

  1. Primordial follicles (immature ovum each) under the ovarian capsule.
  2. At the start of the cycle several enlarge; antrum forms with follicular fluid.
  3. About day 6, one follicle grows rapidly = dominant follicle; others regress (atretic, apoptosis).

Ovulation and ovum transport

  1. About day 14, the distended follicle ruptures; ovum extruded into the abdominal cavity.
  2. Ovum is picked up by the fimbriated ends of the uterine tubes.
  3. Transported to the uterus; unless fertilization occurs, out through the vagina.

Luteal phase

  1. Ruptured follicle fills with blood → corpus hemorrhagicum (minor bleeding may cause mittelschmerz).
  2. Granulosa and theca cells proliferate; clotted blood replaced by yellowish, lipid-rich luteal cells → corpus luteum (blood supply requires VEGF).
  3. Luteal cells secrete estrogen and progesterone.
  4. Pregnancy: corpus luteum persists. No pregnancy: degenerates about day 24 → corpus albicans.

Ova over a lifetime

  1. Fetal ovaries: over 7 million primordial follicles (atresia begins before birth).
  2. Birth: 2 million ova.
  3. Puberty: under 300,000.
  4. Reproductive life: about 500 mature (one per cycle); the remainder degenerate.

Uterine cycle

  1. End of menstruation: only the deep layers of the endometrium remain.
  2. Proliferative phase: estrogens from the developing follicle → regrowth, rapid thickening (day 5–14), glands lengthen.
  3. After ovulation, secretory phase: estrogen + progesterone from the corpus luteum → vascularized, slightly edematous endometrium, coiled glands secreting clear fluid.
  4. Corpus luteum regresses → hormonal support withdrawn.
  5. Endometrium thins → spiral arteries coil more → foci of necrosis coalesce.
  6. Spasm and degeneration of spiral artery walls (vasospasm, probably by prostaglandins) → spotty hemorrhages → confluent → menstrual flow.
  7. If fertilization fails the endometrium is shed and a new cycle starts.

Anovulatory cycle

  1. Ovulation fails → no corpus luteum → no progesterone effect.
  2. Estrogens keep stimulating growth of the proliferative endometrium.
  3. It becomes thick enough to break down and begins to slough → bleeding (variable timing, usually under 28 days; scanty to profuse).

Estrogen synthesis and fate

  1. LH acts on theca interna (cAMP): cholesterol → androstenedione.
  2. Androstenedione (androgens) passes to the granulosa cells.
  3. FSH (cAMP) raises aromatase activity: testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone. Estradiol is secreted into the follicular fluid in primates.
  4. Circulation: 2% free, 60% albumin, 38% GBG.
  5. Liver: glucuronide and sulfate conjugates → urine; bile → reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation).

Progesterone course

  1. Follicular phase: low (about 0.9 ng/mL), small amounts from follicles (theca pregnenolone → granulosa progesterone).
  2. Late follicular phase: secretion begins to increase.
  3. Luteal phase: corpus luteum (LH, adenylyl cyclase, protein synthesis) → peak about 18 ng/mL.
  4. Liver: converted to pregnanediol, conjugated to glucuronic acid, excreted in urine.

Examples

  • Natural estrogens: 17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol.
  • Aromatase conversions: testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone (also in fat, liver, muscle, brain).
  • Binding proteins: albumin, gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG), corticosteroid-binding globulin.
  • Estrogen conjugates (liver): glucuronide and sulfate conjugates.
  • Ovarian structures formed in sequence: corpus hemorrhagicum → corpus luteum → corpus albicans; mittelschmerz as the pain at ovulation.
  • Progesterone metabolite: pregnanediol (conjugated to glucuronic acid).
  • Other mammals: many species ovulate more than one follicle and multiple births are the rule; corpora lutea form in some submammalian species but not in others.
  • Factors affecting menstrual flow: endometrial thickness, medication, diseases that affect the clotting mechanism.
  • Estrogen-dependent body features: breast enlargement, areolar pigmentation, enlargement of uterus and vagina, high-pitched voice, female escutcheon.
  • Androgen-dependent features: pubic and axillary hair growth.
  • Female body configuration also seen in castrate males.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Mittelschmerz: fleeting lower abdominal pain at ovulation from minor bleeding and peritoneal irritation.
  • Abnormal menstrual loss: more than 80 mL is abnormal; menstrual blood does not normally contain clots unless the flow is excessive (fibrinolysin lyses clots).
  • Flow is influenced by endometrial thickness, medication, and diseases affecting clotting.
  • Anovulatory cycles are common for 12–18 months after menarche and before menopause; bleeding is irregular and the flow variable.
  • Postcoital ("morning-after") contraception: large doses of estrogens for 4–6 days after coitus during the fertile period; pregnancy is probably prevented by interference with implantation, not gonadotropin changes.
  • Large doses of progesterone inhibit LH and potentiate estrogen's inhibitory effect, preventing ovulation.
  • Basal body temperature rises at ovulation, probably due to thermogenic progesterone.
  • Large doses of progesterone produce natriuresis (probably blocking aldosterone) and stimulate respiration; no significant anabolic effect.
  • The estrogen-dominated uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin; progesterone decreases this sensitivity.
  • Prostaglandins in the secretory endometrium and menstrual blood are probably responsible for the vasospasm of menstruation.
