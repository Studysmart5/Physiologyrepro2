Overview

Core Idea

Part 2 continues with the ovarian hormones (estrogens, progesterone, and relaxin), then the feedback regulation of ovarian function and the LH surge. It ends with pregnancy: fertilization and implantation, the endocrine changes of pregnancy (hCG, hCS), and parturition with the oxytocin positive feedback loop.

Learning Points

  • Estrogens, progesterone, and relaxin have distinct sources, actions, and clinical relevance.
  • Estrogen exerts negative feedback on LH, but a rising level becomes positive feedback and causes the LH surge and ovulation.
  • Fertilization occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube; the blastocyst implants in the endometrium through the syncytiotrophoblast, and a placenta develops.
  • In pregnancy the corpus luteum is maintained by hCG; the placenta takes over estrogen and progesterone production after the 6th week; hCS acts as the maternal growth hormone of pregnancy.
  • Parturition (about 270 days from fertilization) depends on estrogen, fetal CRH, and an oxytocin positive feedback loop that ends with expulsion of the fetus.

Key Definitions

  • Aromatase (CYP19): enzyme required for estrogen biosynthesis.
  • Relaxin: polypeptide hormone produced in the corpus luteum, uterus, placenta, and mammary glands (women) and the prostate gland (men).
  • LH surge: burst of LH secretion that produces ovulation.
  • Fertilization: union of the ovum and sperm, usually in the ampulla of the uterine tube.
  • Zona pellucida: membranous structure surrounding the ovum; sperm bind to a receptor in it.
  • Acrosomal reaction: breakdown of the acrosome (lysosome-like organelle on the sperm head) with release of enzymes, including the trypsin-like protease acrosin.
  • Fertilin: protein on the surface of the sperm head that mediates fusion with the ovum membrane.
  • Polyspermy: fertilization of the ovum by more than one sperm; prevented by a drop in ovum membrane potential, then by a structural change in the zona pellucida.
  • Blastocyst: the developing embryo as it moves down the tube into the uterus.
  • Syncytiotrophoblast: outer layer of the blastocyst; a multi-nucleate mass with no discernible cell boundaries. Cytotrophoblast: inner layer made of individual cells.
  • Implantation: the blastocyst burrows into the endometrium after the syncytiotrophoblast erodes it.
  • Decidua: the endometrium of pregnancy.
  • Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG): placental glycoprotein gonadotropin made by the syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Human chorionic somatomammotropin (hCS): protein hormone of the syncytiotrophoblast that is lactogenic and has a small amount of growth-stimulating activity.
  • Parturition: birth; the process by which the uterus expels the fetus.

Classification / Types

1. Sources of ovarian and related hormones

  • Estrogens: granulosa cells of ovarian follicles, corpus luteum, placenta.
  • Progesterone: corpus luteum, placenta, and (small amounts) the follicle; small amounts also enter the circulation from testes and adrenal cortex.
  • Relaxin: corpus luteum, uterus, placenta, mammary glands (women); prostate gland (men).

2. Estrogen effects

  • On the female genitalia, on endocrine organs, on the breasts, and on female secondary sex characteristics.

3. Principal targets of progesterone

  • Uterus, breasts, brain.

4. Steps of fertilization

  • (1) Chemoattraction of sperm to the ovum by substances the ovum produces. (2) Adherence to the zona pellucida. (3) Penetration of the zona pellucida and the acrosome reaction. (4) Adherence of the sperm head to the ovum cell membrane, breakdown of the fusion area, and release of the sperm nucleus into the ovum cytoplasm.

5. Placental hormones of pregnancy

  • hCG (luteotropic), hCS (lactogenic, maternal growth-hormone-like), estrogens and progesterone (placenta after week 6).
  • Corpus luteum of pregnancy: estrogens, progesterone, relaxin.

6. Ways oxytocin increases uterine contractions

  • (1) Acts directly on uterine smooth muscle cells. (2) Stimulates formation of prostaglandins in the decidua.

Important Concepts

Estrogens

  • Biosynthesis depends on aromatase (CYP19): testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone (the latter also in fat, liver, muscle, and brain).
  • Theca interna cells have many LH receptors; LH acts via cAMP to increase conversion of cholesterol to androstenedione, which they supply to granulosa cells.
  • Granulosa cells make estradiol when given androgens; in primates it is secreted into the follicular fluid. They have many FSH receptors; FSH acts via cAMP to increase aromatase activity. Mature granulosa cells also acquire LH receptors, and LH stimulates estradiol production.
  • Circulating estradiol: 2% free, 60% bound to albumin, 38% bound to gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG), the same globulin that binds testosterone.
  • In the liver, estradiol, estrone, and estriol are converted to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, excreted in urine; appreciable amounts are secreted in bile and reabsorbed (enterohepatic circulation).
  • Estrogen comes from the ovary with two peaks: just before ovulation and during the midluteal phase. After menopause secretion declines to low levels.
  • Effects on female genitalia: facilitate growth of ovarian follicles; increase motility of uterine tubes; increase uterine blood flow; make uterine smooth muscle more active and excitable, with more frequent action potentials; the "estrogen-dominated" uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin.
  • Effects on endocrine organs: decrease FSH secretion; inhibit LH (negative feedback) in some circumstances and increase LH (positive feedback) in others.
  • Effects on breasts: duct growth; largely responsible for breast enlargement at puberty ("growth hormones of the breast"); areolar pigmentation (usually more intense in the first pregnancy than at puberty).
  • Female secondary sex characteristics (due partly to estrogens, the "feminizing hormones", and partly to absence of testicular androgens): enlargement of breasts, uterus, and vagina; narrow shoulders and broad hips, thighs that converge, arms that diverge (wide carrying angle); larynx retains prepubertal proportions so the voice stays high-pitched; less body hair and more scalp hair; flat-topped pubic hair pattern (female escutcheon); female fat distribution in breasts and buttocks.
  • Growth of pubic and axillary hair in both sexes is due primarily to androgens, not estrogens. The female body configuration is also seen in castrate males.

Progesterone

  • An important intermediate in steroid biosynthesis in all tissues that secrete steroid hormones.
  • Circulating progesterone: 2% free, 80% bound to albumin, 18% bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin. Short half-life; converted in the liver to pregnanediol, conjugated to glucuronic acid and excreted in urine.
  • In the follicle, theca cells provide pregnenolone to granulosa cells, which convert it to progesterone. Secretion begins to rise late in the follicular phase; the corpus luteum produces large quantities in the luteal phase.
  • LH stimulates progesterone secretion by the corpus luteum via activation of adenylyl cyclase and a later step dependent on protein synthesis.
  • Actions: responsible for progestational changes in the endometrium and cyclical changes in the cervix and vagina; antiestrogenic effect on myometrial cells (decreases excitability, sensitivity to oxytocin, and spontaneous electrical activity; increases membrane potential); decreases the number of endometrial estrogen receptors and increases conversion of 17β-estradiol to less active estrogens.
  • Breast: stimulates development of lobules and alveoli; induces differentiation of estrogen-prepared ductal tissue; supports secretory function during lactation.
  • Feedback effects are complex, at hypothalamic and pituitary levels. Large doses inhibit LH and potentiate estrogen's inhibitory effect, preventing ovulation.
  • It is thermogenic (probably responsible for the rise in basal body temperature at ovulation), stimulates respiration and alveolar PCO2, and in large doses produces natriuresis, probably by blocking aldosterone action on the kidney. No significant anabolic effect.

Relaxin

  • In pregnancy: relaxes the pubic symphysis and other pelvic joints and softens and dilates the uterine cervix, facilitating delivery. Also inhibits uterine contractions and may play a role in mammary gland development.
  • Nonpregnant women: found in the corpus luteum and in the endometrium during the secretory but not the proliferative phase; function unknown.
  • Men: found in semen; may help maintain sperm motility and aid sperm penetration of the ovum.

Feedback regulation of ovarian function

  • Early follicular phase: inhibin B low, FSH modestly elevated, fostering follicular growth. LH is held in check by negative feedback of rising plasma estrogen.
  • 36–48 h before ovulation: estrogen feedback becomes positive, initiating the LH surge that produces ovulation. Ovulation occurs about 9 h after the LH peak.
  • FSH also peaks despite a small rise in inhibin, probably because of strong stimulation of gonadotropes by GnRH.
  • Luteal phase: LH and FSH are low because of elevated estrogen, progesterone, and inhibin.
  • A moderate, constant circulating estrogen level exerts negative feedback on LH; an elevated level during the cycle exerts positive feedback. How negative feedback switches to positive and back to negative in the luteal phase remains unknown.

Fertilization and implantation

  • Fertilization usually occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube. Millions of sperm are deposited in the vagina; eventually 50–100 reach the ovum and many contact the zona pellucida.
  • Sperm bind to a receptor in the zona, followed by the acrosomal reaction (release of enzymes including acrosin). Acrosin facilitates but is not required for penetration of the zona.
  • When one sperm reaches the ovum membrane, fusion is mediated by fertilin. The fusion provides the signal that initiates development and reduces the ovum's membrane potential, which prevents polyspermy. A later structural change in the zona pellucida gives longer-term protection.
  • The embryo, now a blastocyst, travels down the tube to the uterus in about 3 days, reaching the 8- or 16-cell stage.
  • On contact with the endometrium the blastocyst has an outer syncytiotrophoblast and an inner cytotrophoblast. The syncytiotrophoblast erodes the endometrium and the blastocyst burrows in (implantation), usually on the dorsal wall of the uterus. A placenta then develops and the trophoblast remains associated with it.

Endocrine changes of pregnancy

  • In all mammals the corpus luteum at fertilization fails to regress and enlarges under placental gonadotropins; in humans this is hCG.
  • The enlarged corpus luteum secretes estrogens, progesterone, and relaxin. Progesterone and relaxin help maintain pregnancy by inhibiting myometrial contractions; progesterone prevents prostaglandin production by the uterus, which stops contractions.
  • After the 6th week the placenta produces enough estrogen and progesterone (from maternal and fetal precursors) to take over the corpus luteum's function.
  • Corpus luteum function begins to decline after 8 weeks but it persists throughout pregnancy. hCG secretion falls after an initial marked rise, while estrogen and progesterone keep rising until just before parturition.

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)

  • A glycoprotein (contains galactose and hexosamine) produced by the syncytiotrophoblast.
  • Like pituitary glycoprotein hormones it has α and β subunits; hCG-α is identical to the α subunit of LH, FSH, and TSH. Molecular weight: hCG-α 18,000, hCG-β 28,000.
  • Primarily luteinizing and luteotropic with little FSH activity.
  • Measured by radioimmunoassay; detectable in blood as early as 6 days after conception. Its presence in urine is the basis of pregnancy tests, sometimes detectable as early as 14 days after conception.

Human chorionic somatomammotropin (hCS)

  • Protein hormone secreted in large amounts by the syncytiotrophoblast; lactogenic with a small growth-stimulating activity.
  • Structure is very similar to human growth hormone; GH, hCS, and prolactin appear to have evolved from a common progenitor hormone.
  • Large quantities in maternal blood, but very little reaches the fetus. Maternal pituitary GH secretion is not increased in pregnancy and may be decreased by hCS.
  • Acts as the "maternal growth hormone of pregnancy": nitrogen, potassium, and calcium retention, lipolysis, and decreased glucose utilization; the last two divert glucose to the fetus.

Parturition

  • Pregnancy averages 270 days from fertilization (284 days from the first day of the menstrual period preceding conception). Irregular uterine contractions increase in frequency in the last month.
  • The cervix is firm until near delivery, then softens and dilates while the body of the uterus contracts and expels the fetus. The mechanisms that start labor remain uncertain.
  • Estrogens (from increased circulating DHEAS) make the uterus more excitable, increase gap junctions between myometrial cells, and increase prostaglandin production, causing contractions.
  • CRH from the fetal hypothalamus rises and is supplemented by placental CRH → more fetal ACTH → more cortisol, which hastens maturation of the respiratory system. In a sense the fetus picks the time to be born by increasing CRH secretion.
  • Oxytocin receptors in the myometrium and decidua increase more than 100-fold in pregnancy and peak in early labor. Estrogens raise receptor number; uterine distension late in pregnancy may also increase their formation.
  • Once labor starts, contractions dilate the cervix, which sends signals in afferent nerves that increase oxytocin secretion, creating a positive feedback loop that ends when the products of conception are expelled.
  • Oxytocin acts directly on uterine smooth muscle and stimulates prostaglandin formation in the decidua; prostaglandins enhance oxytocin-induced contractions. During labor spinal reflexes and voluntary abdominal muscle contractions ("bearing down") also aid delivery.

Processes / Stages

Estrogen synthesis

LH on theca interna (cAMP) → cholesterol → androstenedione → to granulosa cells → FSH (cAMP) raises aromatase → estradiol (testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone) → liver conjugation → urine; bile → enterohepatic circulation.

Hormonal feedback through the cycle

  1. Early follicular: inhibin B low, FSH modestly elevated → follicular growth; estrogen rises and restrains LH.
  2. 36–48 h before ovulation: positive feedback → LH surge (FSH also peaks).
  3. Ovulation about 9 h after the LH peak.
  4. Luteal: LH and FSH low (high estrogen, progesterone, inhibin).

Fertilization to implantation

  1. Chemoattraction of sperm to the ovum.
  2. Adherence to the zona pellucida (receptor binding).
  3. Acrosomal reaction (acrosin) and penetration of the zona.
  4. Sperm head binds the ovum membrane (fertilin); fusion, release of the sperm nucleus into the cytoplasm.
  5. Membrane potential falls (block to polyspermy), then the zona pellucida changes structurally.
  6. Blastocyst travels down the tube (about 3 days, 8–16 cells).
  7. Contact with the endometrium: syncytiotrophoblast (outer) and cytotrophoblast (inner) → erosion and implantation (dorsal wall) → placenta.

Hormonal course of pregnancy

  1. Fertilization: corpus luteum does not regress and enlarges under hCG.
  2. Corpus luteum secretes estrogens, progesterone, relaxin; contractions inhibited.
  3. After week 6 the placenta takes over estrogen and progesterone production.
  4. After 8 weeks corpus luteum function declines; hCG falls after an early rise.
  5. Estrogen and progesterone rise until just before parturition.

Onset of labor and oxytocin positive feedback

  1. Estrogen (DHEAS-derived) and fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol (lung maturation) prepare the uterus and fetus.
  2. Oxytocin receptors increase more than 100-fold; prostaglandins rise.
  3. Uterine contractions → dilation of the cervix and distention of the vagina.
  4. Stimuli from cervix and vagina (afferent nerves) → increased oxytocin secretion.
  5. More contractions (directly and via prostaglandins) → loop repeats until expulsion of the products of conception.

Examples

  • Natural estrogens: 17β-estradiol, estrone, estriol.
  • Aromatase conversions: testosterone → estradiol; androstenedione → estrone (also in fat, liver, muscle, brain).
  • Gonadal polypeptide: inhibin B (inhibits FSH). Polypeptide hormone: relaxin.
  • Progesterone metabolite: pregnanediol (conjugated to glucuronic acid, excreted in urine).
  • Binding proteins: albumin, gonadal steroid binding globulin (GBG), corticosteroid-binding globulin.
  • Sperm and zona proteins: acrosin (trypsin-like protease), fertilin.
  • Glycoprotein hormones sharing the α subunit with hCG: LH, FSH, TSH.
  • Hormones related to GH: hCS (and prolactin, from a common progenitor).
  • Pregnancy hormones: hCG, hCS, estrogens, progesterone, relaxin.
  • Mediators of labor: oxytocin, prostaglandins, estrogens (from DHEAS), fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol.

Clinical / Practical Importance

  • Postcoital ("morning-after") contraception: large doses of estrogens for 4–6 days after coitus during the fertile period; pregnancy is probably prevented by interference with implantation rather than changes in gonadotropin secretion.
  • Large doses of progesterone inhibit LH secretion and potentiate estrogen's inhibitory effect, preventing ovulation.
  • Basal body temperature rises at ovulation, probably due to thermogenic progesterone.
  • Relaxin relaxes pelvic joints and softens and dilates the cervix, facilitating delivery; in men it may help sperm motility and penetration.
  • Progesterone large doses cause natriuresis (probably blocking aldosterone); it has no significant anabolic effect.
  • The estrogen-dominated uterus is more sensitive to oxytocin; progesterone reduces this sensitivity.
  • Pregnancy tests rely on hCG in urine (as early as 14 days after conception); blood hCG is detectable by radioimmunoassay as early as 6 days.
  • Low hCS levels are a sign of placental insufficiency.
  • hCS diverts glucose to the fetus (decreased maternal glucose utilization, lipolysis) while retaining nitrogen, potassium, and calcium.
  • Cortisol raised by fetal CRH and ACTH hastens maturation of the fetal respiratory system.
  • Oxytocin and prostaglandins drive labor through a positive feedback loop; spinal reflexes and "bearing down" aid delivery.
  • Progesterone and relaxin help maintain pregnancy by inhibiting myometrial contractions.

Exam Focus

  • Granulosa vs theca interna: receptors, androstenedione, aromatase (CYP19).
  • Estrogens: three types, sources, plasma binding (2% / 60% albumin / 38% GBG), secretion rates (36, 380, 250 µg/day; men 50), two peaks, all effects (genitalia, endocrine, breasts, secondary sex characteristics).
  • Progesterone: sources, binding (2% / 80% albumin / 18% CBG), pregnanediol, plasma levels (0.3, 0.9, 18 ng/mL), actions (antiestrogenic, thermogenic, natriuresis, no anabolic effect), LH inhibition at large doses.
  • Relaxin: sources, actions in pregnancy, nonpregnant women, and men.
  • Feedback: early follicular (low inhibin B, FSH modestly raised), estrogen positive feedback 36–48 h before ovulation, LH surge, ovulation about 9 h after the LH peak, luteal suppression; the switching mechanism is unknown.
  • Morning-after contraception: high-dose estrogens 4–6 days, probably blocks implantation.
  • Fertilization: site (ampulla), the 4 steps, zona pellucida, acrosomal reaction and acrosin (not required), fertilin, block to polyspermy (membrane potential, then zona change); 50–100 sperm reach the ovum.
  • Blastocyst: about 3 days, 8–16 cells; syncytiotrophoblast vs cytotrophoblast; implantation on the dorsal wall; placenta.
  • Pregnancy endocrinology: hCG maintains the corpus luteum; placenta takes over after week 6; corpus luteum declines after 8 weeks; estrogen and progesterone rise until parturition; progesterone and relaxin inhibit contractions.
  • hCG: glycoprotein, α/β subunits (α same as LH, FSH, TSH), MW 18,000 / 28,000, luteotropic, detected in blood at 6 days and urine at 14 days.
  • hCS: lactogenic, GH-like, "maternal growth hormone of pregnancy", effects on N, K, Ca, lipolysis, glucose; low levels = placental insufficiency.
  • Parturition: 270 vs 284 days; cervix vs uterine body; estrogen from DHEAS (gap junctions, prostaglandins); fetal CRH → ACTH → cortisol → lung maturation; oxytocin receptors rise over 100-fold; positive feedback loop of oxytocin; two ways oxytocin acts (direct, prostaglandins in decidua).
